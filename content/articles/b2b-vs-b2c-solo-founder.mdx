## The Fun Choice Is Not Always the Kind Choice

Consumer apps look fun.

They feel lighter. They make better screenshots. Your friends understand them in five seconds.

B2B products feel boring until you look at the math.

A solo founder does not need more romance. You need a wedge where the price, churn, and sales motion give you room to breathe.

If you are still choosing what to build, start with a narrow problem from the [startup ideas library](/startup-ideas), then decide whether that problem belongs in B2B or B2C.

## Myth 1: B2C Is Easier Because Everyone Is a User

This sounds true because everyone is a consumer.

Your cousin uses apps. Your friends use apps. You use apps. So the market feels close.

But reach is not the same as demand.

B2C usually makes you win attention before you win revenue. You need people to care, sign up, return, invite friends, and eventually pay. Each step leaks.

B2B starts with a smaller room.

That is the advantage.

You can name the buyer. You can find the pain. You can say, "This saves your team three hours a week," and the person on the other side already knows what an hour costs.

### The Solo Founder Test

Ask this:

- Can I name 50 people who feel this pain at work?
- Can I reach them without paid ads?
- Would solving it save money, time, risk, or revenue?

If yes, B2B deserves a serious look.

If no, you may be building a consumer habit, not a business.

## Myth 2: You Need Millions of Users

You only need millions of users when the product is cheap and churny.

That is the trap in many consumer ideas.

A B2C app at $5 to $50 per month can work, but the math gets demanding fast. If most people pay $10 per month, $1M ARR means roughly 8,333 paying subscribers.

That is not impossible.

It is just a different game.

A B2B product at $50 to $500 per month changes the shape of the problem. At $200 per month, $1M ARR is roughly 417 customers. At $500 per month, it is 167 customers.

Go higher into teams, agencies, clinics, operators, or compliance-heavy niches, and you may need dozens of customers instead of thousands.

This is why so many strong solo founder ideas look boring from the outside.

They do not need virality. They need clear pain and a buyer with a budget.

When you browse [startup ideas](/startup-ideas), pay attention to who already spends money to solve the problem badly. That is usually the better clue than how cool the app sounds.

## Myth 3: B2B Means Enterprise Sales

B2B does not have to mean procurement, security questionnaires, and six-month sales cycles.

That is enterprise.

Solo founders can play in smaller B2B:

- Freelancers who need client admin off their plate
- Agencies that repeat the same reporting work every week
- Local service businesses that lose leads in messy inboxes
- Creators with sponsorship, content, or operations workflows
- Internal operators using spreadsheets as duct tape

These buyers can often pay $50 to $500 per month without a board meeting.

They still want trust. They still ask questions. But they are not all enterprise buyers.

Your job is to avoid pretending every business customer is a Fortune 500 account.

Start where the buyer can swipe a card.

## The Churn Difference Is Brutal

Churn is where the B2C dream often gets punched in the face.

Consumer subscriptions commonly churn 5% to 15% monthly. That means every month you are refilling a bucket with a hole in it.

B2B churn is often discussed annually, not monthly. A small B2B SaaS might see roughly 3% to 10% annual churn when it solves a real workflow problem.

That difference changes your calendar.

With high monthly churn, you are always feeding acquisition.

With lower annual churn, each customer becomes more durable. Support matters. Onboarding matters. The product still has to work. But you are not restarting from zero every month.

This is why the "boring" product can feel calmer to operate.

## CAC Is Not Just Ad Spend

Consumer founders often say, "I'll post on TikTok."

Maybe you will.

But content is still CAC. Your time is CAC. The 30 videos that did not hit are CAC. The influencer tests are CAC. The paid experiments are CAC.

B2B acquisition can be slower, but it can also be more direct.

You can build a list of 100 specific buyers. You can send useful outreach. You can demo the product. You can ask what would make it worth paying for.

That is not glamorous.

It is founder-led sales, and it is one of the few channels a solo founder can run without raising money.

## When B2C Still Wins

B2C is not wrong.

It wins when the product has a daily or weekly habit, a clear emotional pull, and a distribution loop that is native to the product.

Good B2C wedges often have one of these:

- A community identity people want to share
- A personal pain that feels urgent every week
- A creation loop where users publish what they make
- A low-friction referral moment
- A huge existing audience you already know how to reach

If you have that, B2C can be the right move.

But do not pick B2C because you are scared of selling.

That is not strategy. That is avoidance with nicer branding.

## How to Pick Without Lying to Yourself

Use this simple rule:

If the problem costs a business time, money, leads, compliance risk, or operational pain, start B2B.

If the problem creates a strong personal habit, identity, or entertainment loop, consider B2C.

Then pressure-test the idea:

- Who pays?
- How much do they already spend?
- How often does the pain happen?
- Can you reach 50 buyers this week?
- Would the product still matter after the novelty fades?

If you cannot answer those, you are not choosing a market. You are choosing a vibe.

## Pick One Wedge

The worst move is building a product that says "for individuals and teams" before it has one paying user.

Pick one.

One buyer.

One pain.

One price.

One outreach motion.

You can expand later. You cannot learn clearly when every conversation points in a different direction.

Start with a scoped problem in the [startup ideas library](/startup-ideas). Pick one wedge. Then write the landing page as if only that buyer exists.

## TL;DR

- B2C feels easier because the users are familiar.
- B2B is often easier to monetize because the buyer already has a budget.
- B2B pricing often sits around $50 to $500 per month, while B2C often sits around $5 to $50.
- B2C churn can be 5% to 15% monthly, while strong B2B churn is often discussed annually.
- You do not need enterprise sales to sell to businesses.
- B2C still wins when you have habit, identity, and distribution.

If you want the kinder default, choose B2B first. Then grab a specific wedge from [startup ideas](/startup-ideas) and build for one buyer this weekend.
