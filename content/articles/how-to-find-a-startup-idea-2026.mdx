## Quick Answer

You don't find a startup idea by brainstorming. You find one by going where people already complain in writing — negative reviews, subreddit threads, freelance job boards, support forums — and looking for the same problem stated repeatedly by people who already spend money on it. Brainstorming produces ideas you like. Mining produces ideas someone already tried to pay to solve.

That distinction matters more than any other decision you'll make. CB Insights' analysis of startup post-mortems puts **"no market need" at 42%** — the single largest cause of failure, ahead of running out of cash at 29%. Nearly half of dead startups built something correctly that nobody wanted.

This article covers six hunting grounds and a scoring system. If you'd rather skip the hunt entirely, the [startup ideas library](/startup-ideas) is pre-mined signal — problems already sourced, sized, and written up as buildable weekend projects.

## Why Brainstorming Fails

Brainstorming samples from what you already know. That's the whole problem — your imagination is bounded by your exposure, and your exposure is mostly software that already exists.

It also selects for the wrong thing. In a brainstorm, ideas win because they sound exciting. In a market, ideas win because someone is bleeding money or hours right now. Those two filters rarely agree.

The fix is mechanical: stop generating, start collecting. Every method below is a way of reading other people's pain instead of inventing your own.

## The 6 Hunting Grounds

### 1. Negative Reviews (Highest Signal Per Minute)

Pick a category of software you understand. Open the review pages — G2, Capterra, the App Store, Shopify's app store, Chrome Web Store — and read **50 one- and two-star reviews**.

You are not looking for "it crashed." You're looking for structural complaints: the missing feature, the workflow the tool refuses to support, the pricing tier that punishes small teams.

The complaints are pre-extracted problems. You skip the entire discovery step because a frustrated user already did the writing for you. Tally what repeats. The most common structural complaint in a category is a product.

**Why it works:** these people already pay for software in this category. Willingness to pay is proven before you write a line of code.

### 2. Subreddit Phrase Hunting

Go to a subreddit for an industry, not a technology. `r/smallbusiness`, `r/dentistry`, `r/propertymanagement`, `r/videography` — anywhere a trade gathers.

Search these exact phrases:

- "I wish there was"
- "is there an app for"
- "alternative to"
- "how do you all handle"
- "still doing this manually"

Read the top 30 results for each. Patterns emerge fast — usually within an hour you'll see the same three or four operational headaches surface across unrelated threads.

The last phrase is the most valuable. "Still doing this manually" is someone describing an unautomated workflow they already perform. That's a weekend MVP with a customer attached.

### 3. Upwork and Fiverr Demand Signals

Freelance marketplaces are the cleanest demand data on the internet, because every listing is a person who has already opened their wallet.

The heuristic: **if a task is posted 50+ times a month at $200+ per job, it's productizable.** Someone is paying repeatedly for a manual service that follows a predictable pattern. Patterns become software.

Search job categories in a niche you understand. Note which requests are nearly identical across postings. Identical requests mean a standardized workflow — which means a tool can do most of it.

### 4. Your Own Last Job

Not "solve your own problem" in the consumer sense. Specifically: the spreadsheet you maintained, the report you assembled every Friday, the handoff between two teams that always broke.

Internal workflow pain is the least competitive idea space in software, because the people who experience it usually can't build and the people who can build have never experienced it. You may be the only person who has both.

Write down every recurring task from your last role that took over 30 minutes and involved copying data between two systems. That list is an idea backlog.

### 5. Bridging Two Worlds

Some of the strongest opportunities come from applying a solved pattern in one industry to an industry that hasn't seen it yet.

Subscription billing was solved in SaaS a decade before it reached car washes. Automated appointment reminders were normal in dentistry years before they were normal in home services. Scheduling, dispatch, review collection, digital intake forms — these are solved problems that are still unsolved in dozens of trades.

Ask: what's standard in tech that would look like magic in an industry my cousin works in?

### 6. Where Incumbents Are Weak on Purpose

Large companies deliberately abandon segments. Too small, too custom, too much support burden per dollar.

Look for categories where the market leader's cheapest plan starts at several hundred dollars a month, or where their onboarding requires a sales call. Everyone below that line is unserved and knows it. They're currently using a spreadsheet and resenting it.

You don't need to beat the incumbent. You need to serve the customer they declined.

## Scoring an Idea Before You Build

Collecting gives you a list. Scoring tells you which one to spend a weekend on. Rate each candidate 1–5 on five dimensions:

| Dimension | The question | Red flag |
|-----------|--------------|----------|
| **Pain frequency** | How often does this hurt? | Annually |
| **Existing spend** | What do they pay today to cope? | Nothing |
| **Reachability** | Can you find 20 of these people this week? | "Everyone" |
| **Buildable core** | Can the useful 20% ship in a weekend? | Needs integrations first |
| **Your edge** | Do you know this world? | Read about it once |

Anything scoring under 15 total goes back on the shelf. Anything scoring 20+ goes to validation.

The dimension people fudge most is **existing spend**. If the honest answer is "they currently do nothing," you're not selling a solution — you're selling a behavior change, which costs ten times more to market.

The dimension people ignore most is **reachability**. A great idea for a group you cannot locate is a hobby. Before building, confirm there is a subreddit, a Facebook group, a trade association, a conference, or a directory where these people are already gathered.

## Turning a Candidate Into a Tested Idea

Scoring is still theory. Two moves convert it into evidence, and both fit in an evening:

**Ten cold conversations.** Not pitches — questions about the last time they hit the problem. Ask what they did instead, what it cost, and what they'd tried before. If nobody can recall a specific recent instance, the pain isn't real.

**A landing page with a price.** Not a waitlist for "early access." A page describing the outcome with a number attached. Interest is cheap; a click on a pricing button is a signal.

Do those two things and you'll kill 80% of your list in a week — which is the point. The goal isn't to protect your favorite idea. It's to spend the weekend on the one that survives contact.

## What If Nothing Sticks

**Everything feels taken.** Good — competition is proof of demand. Narrow instead of abandoning. "Invoicing" is taken. "Invoicing for freelance translators who bill per word" is not.

**You keep finding ideas you don't care about.** Filter your hunting grounds by industries you'd tolerate talking about for two years. Interest is a real constraint, just not the first one.

**You find problems but they're too big.** Every large problem has a first painful step. Ship the step, not the platform.

**You can't tell if the pain is real.** You're missing conversations, not analysis. Ten calls beats ten more hours of research every time.

## FAQ

### How long should finding a startup idea take?

A focused weekend of mining usually produces 15–30 candidates, and scoring narrows that to two or three worth testing. If you've spent a month searching, you're brainstorming, not mining.

### Is it bad if someone is already building this?

No. An existing competitor confirms demand and saves you the cost of educating a market. Failure from "no market need" is far more common than failure from competition.

### Do I need original market research?

Not to start. Public complaints, reviews, and job listings are primary sources — real people describing real problems in their own words, at no cost.

### Should I use AI to generate ideas?

Use it to summarize and cluster the raw complaints you've collected, not to invent ideas from scratch. Generated ideas tend to converge on generic categories with no specific buyer attached.
