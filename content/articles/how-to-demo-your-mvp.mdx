## This Is for You If Your Demo Gets Polite Replies

You built the MVP.

You sent the link.

People said, "Looks cool."

Then nothing happened.

That usually means the product is not being demonstrated in the language of the buyer. You are showing features. They are trying to decide whether this is worth time, trust, and money.

Before you record another walkthrough, pick a tight problem from the [startup ideas library](/startup-ideas) and make sure your demo sells one outcome to one type of person.

## Do Not Put the Loom in Email One

This is the mistake.

You write a cold email with five sentences, paste a Loom link, and ask the person to watch it.

That sounds efficient to you.

It feels like homework to them.

Email one should earn permission, not demand attention.

Keep it short:

- Show you understand their role
- Name the problem
- Ask if they want the 90-second walkthrough

That is it.

The goal is not to demo yet. The goal is to get a yes from someone with a real reason to care.

## Use the Permission-First Pattern

Here is the simple flow.

### Touch 1: Ask

Send a plain note.

"I built a small tool that helps agencies turn messy client updates into a clean weekly status email. Worth sending you the 90-second walkthrough?"

No deck.

No calendar link.

No giant paragraph.

### Touch 2: Send

If they say yes, send the Loom.

Add one sentence that frames what they are about to see:

"Watch for the before-and-after at the 40-second mark. That is the whole product."

### Touch 3: Convert

After they watch, ask a buyer question.

"If this saved your team two hours per week, would it be worth $49 per month?"

That question is more useful than "What do you think?"

"What do you think?" invites opinions.

"Would you pay?" creates evidence.

## Keep the Demo Between 90 Seconds and 3 Minutes

Your MVP demo is not a product tour.

It is a sales asset.

That means it has one job: help the buyer understand the pain, the outcome, and the next step.

Use this timing:

- 0 to 15 seconds: who it is for
- 15 to 45 seconds: the painful before state
- 45 to 90 seconds: the product producing the outcome
- 90 to 150 seconds: why it matters
- Final 10 seconds: one clear CTA

If you cannot explain it in three minutes, the product may be too broad.

Go back to [startup ideas](/startup-ideas), pick a narrower wedge, and demo that.

## The 90-Second Script

Use this exact structure.

### 1. Problem

"If you run a small agency, client updates are probably scattered across Slack, email, and project tools."

Name the painful mess.

Do not start with your logo.

Do not start with "I'm excited to show you."

Start with the buyer's current reality.

### 2. Outcome

"This turns those scattered updates into one client-ready weekly email in under two minutes."

That is the promise.

Make it concrete. Time saved. Revenue protected. Error avoided. Lead captured. Decision made.

### 3. Walkthrough

Show the shortest path from input to output.

For most weekend MVPs, that means:

- Paste or connect the messy thing
- Click the one important action
- Show the useful result

Skip settings. Skip edge cases. Skip the roadmap.

### 4. CTA

"If this would help, reply with one workflow you'd want it to handle and I'll send you access."

Or:

"If you want to try it, book a 15-minute setup call here."

One next step.

Not three.

## Live Demo Rules

Live demos are better when the buyer is already warm.

Do not use a live call to explain what the product is. Use it to prove that it works for their world.

Before the call:

- Ask for one real example they can share
- Open the app and test your demo path
- Close every tab you do not need
- Prepare a backup Loom in case the app breaks

During the call:

- Restate the problem in their words
- Use their data if possible
- Pause after the output appears
- Ask, "Is this close to what you would need?"

Then be quiet.

The pause is where the useful feedback shows up.

## Follow Up Within 2 Hours

The demo is not done when the call ends.

Send the follow-up while the pain is still fresh.

Use this format:

- What they cared about
- What you will change or send
- The buying or trial next step

Example:

"You said the biggest pain is turning Friday client notes into Monday updates. I added that as the default template. Want me to set up a trial workspace for your next update cycle?"

That is specific.

Specific beats polished.

## Use a 48-Hour Follow-Up Window

If they do not reply, follow up once within 48 hours.

Keep it useful.

"Quick follow-up. Based on what you shared, the main fit is weekly client reporting, not general project management. Want me to send a trial link for that use case?"

If they still do not reply, move on.

You are not trying to win one silent prospect. You are trying to learn from a market.

## When to Use Cal.com

Do not send a booking link before the buyer cares.

Send it after one of these happens:

- They ask how it works
- They say the pain is real
- They watched the Loom
- They replied with a workflow

Then make the CTA clear:

"If it is useful, grab 15 minutes here and I'll show you using your own workflow."

Cal.com is not the offer.

The outcome is the offer.

## A Simple Demo Checklist

Before sending any demo, check this:

- Does the first sentence name the buyer?
- Does the demo show one painful before state?
- Does it reach the outcome fast?
- Is it under three minutes?
- Is there one CTA?
- Does the follow-up ask for money, access, or a real next step?

If no, cut more.

## TL;DR

- Do not put Loom in the first cold email.
- Ask permission first.
- Keep async demos between 90 seconds and 3 minutes.
- Use problem, outcome, walkthrough, CTA.
- Follow up within 2 hours after a live demo.
- Send one 48-hour follow-up if they go quiet.
- Use Cal.com only after they show interest.

Your demo should not explain every feature. It should make one person believe one outcome is worth paying for. Start with a buyer-specific idea from [startup ideas](/startup-ideas), demo the outcome, and ask for the next step.
